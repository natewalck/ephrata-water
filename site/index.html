Bacteria testing across town

The authority samples about 25 fixed sites around the system every few weeks for total coliform (harmless bacteria that signal something may have gotten into the pipes) and E. coli (a sign of fecal contamination, which is a health concern). Any positive result requires three repeat samples within 24 hours: at the same tap, and at neighbors upstream and downstream.

Chemicals with a federal health limit

Each bar shows the highest result in treated water compared with the EPA maximum contaminant level. Under 100% means the water met the limit.

Taste, odor, and staining guidelines

These are EPA’s “secondary” standards: they cover how water looks and tastes, not whether it is safe to drink. Water is shown before and after treatment at each plant so you can see what treatment removes.

Water that is not tap water

The lab packet also covers untreated creek and well water and the salty waste stream from the Well #4 filtration process. These are shown for completeness; none of them reach a tap.

What was tested, and what they found

Lead is measured at household taps every few years. EPA’s “action level” is the point where a utility must take corrective action, and results are reported as the 90th percentile of the samples collected.

PFAS and other unregulated contaminants

In 2024–2025, EPA required most water systems to test for 29 PFAS (“forever chemicals”) and lithium. These are the measured results for this system’s treated water.

Violations on record, in plain English

EPA keeps roughly ten years of history. A “violation” is any missed requirement — most are paperwork (a late test or report), not unsafe water. Health-based violations, where a measured level actually exceeded a legal limit, are marked distinctly.

    What the public can and cannot see

    The only measured results published in machine-readable federal data are lead and copper (every few years) and the 2024–25 PFAS survey above. Routine results for everything else — bacteria, disinfection byproducts, nitrates and more — are reported to regulators but not published anywhere a program can read: Pennsylvania’s open-data feed of sample results has not been updated since March 2021, and the authority’s own reports are not machine-readable. The lab results above were obtained by a Right-to-Know Law request to Ephrata Borough and cover a single sampling window; they are not an ongoing feed.

    About this data

    Ephrata Borough’s tap water comes from the Ephrata Area Joint Authority (system ID PA7360045), drawn roughly half from Cocalico Creek and half from groundwater wells.
